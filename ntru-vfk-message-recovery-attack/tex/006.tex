\section{Introduction}
The NTRU cryptosystem was created by Hoffstein, Pipher, and Silverman in 1996 as a means of encryption and signature. Its security is based on the difficulty of solving a system of linear equations over polynomial rings, a problem that is expected to remain hard even with quantum computers. While it is not equivalent to the Shortest and Closest Vector Problems (SVP/CVP) in lattices, the NTRU cryptosystem has withstood over 20 years of cryptanalysis and variants of it have been shown to be secure under the Ring Learning With Error (R-LWE) hardness assumption. NTRU is known for its exceptional performance and moderate key-size, making it a popular choice for embedded cryptography. It has been standardized by IEEE, X9.98, PQCRYPTO, and was a finalist in the NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization effort. Ultimately, the Kyber algorithm was selected for standardization. It is also noted in the  3rd Status Report of NIST for PQC\footnote{\url{https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2022/NIST.IR.8413.pdf}}, the overall performance of any of these KEMs (i.e. Kyber, Saber and NTRU) would be acceptable for general-use applications. Also, NTRU-HRSS was chosen by Google for their ALTS protocol (ALTS:Application Layer Transport Security) in the hybrid model\footnote{\url{https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/why-google-now-uses-post-quantum-cryptography-for-internal-comms}}.

\subsection{Relevant work}

The NTRU cryptosystem was first targeted for a lattice attack in 1997 by Coppersmith \cite{CopSha97}. Later, Gentry developed an effective strategy, particularly useful when $N$ is composite, see \cite{gentry}. May, in \cite{May}, used his own class of lattices, called run-lattices, to solve similar issues. 


Silverman \cite{Silverman} built on May's idea and proposed a technique that involves choosing $r$ coefficients and reducing the dimension of the lattice to make them zero. A similar approach was taken by the authors of \cite{Gama-Nguyen}, who leveraged decryption failures to recover the secret key if the decryption oracle supported it.

Other techniques have been developed, such as reducing the NTRU problem to a multivariate quadratic system over a finite field with two elements using Witt vectors \cite{gerald,witt}. Odlyzko \cite{Odlyzko} proposed a meet-in-the-middle attack that divides the search space into two parts, resulting in lower time complexity but high memory requirements. Howgrave's hybrid attack \cite{hybrid attack} combines lattice reduction with a meet-in-the-middle algorithm, and has been widely studied by researchers to evaluate the security of lattice-based encryption methods.

To attack the Ntru cryptosystem with a higher modulus than that in the NTRUEncrypt standard, similar methods were proposed independently by Albrecht, Bai, and Ducas \cite{Albrecht} and Cheon, Jeong, and Lee \cite{Cheon}. Kirchner \cite{Kirchner} demonstrated that time complexity is polynomial for $q=2^{\mathrm {\Omega }(\sqrt{n\log \log n})}$ in $\mathbb {Q}(\zeta _{2^n}).$ Lastly, Nguyen \cite{Nguyen - Boosting the hybrid} enhanced and clarified the hybrid and meet-in-the-middle attacks. The subfield attack variation proposed in this paper is superior to the earlier methods, but not as effective as the hybrid attack.


\subsection{Our contribution}
Firstly, we replace the approximate CVP-oracle of \cite{adam_draz} with an actual CVP-oracle, which allows for more precise and better results of our attack. Additionally, our attack is also extended to other NTRU variations such as NTRU-prime. Moreover, when we transfer our attack to VFK type NTRU lattices, we observe an improvement in the simplest Babai attack. This is particularly evident in \texttt{ntruhps2048509}, where our method is shown to be superior to Babai's attack in the same lattice. These advancements significantly  enhances the previous attack \cite{adam_draz}. What is more, our research has uncovered a non-trivial connection between NTRU lattice and a VFK lattice, which further enhances our understanding of these encryption systems.

\section{Lattices}\label{sec:lattices}

In this section, we recall some well-known facts about lattices which form the 
background to our algorithms.

Let ${{\bf{b}}_1,{\bf{b}}_2,\ldots,{\bf{b}}_n}$ linearly independent vectors of 
${\mathbb{R}}^{m}$.
The set 
\[\mathcal{L} = \bigg{\{} \sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_j{\bf{b}}_j :
 \alpha_j\in\mathbb{Z}, 1\leq j\leq n\bigg{\}}\]
is called  a {\em lattice} and 
the set $\mathcal{B} = \{{\bf{b}}_1,\ldots,{\bf{b}}_n\}$ a basis of 
$\mathcal{L}$. 
All the bases of $\mathcal{L}$ have the same number of elements $n$ which is  called
 {\em dimension} or {\em rank} of $\mathcal{L}$. If $n=m$, then
the lattice $\mathcal{L}$ is said to have {\em full rank}. 
We denote by $M$ the $n\times m$-matrix  having as rows the vectors 
${\bf{b}}_1,\ldots,{\bf{b}}_n$. 
If $\mathcal{L}$ has full rank, then the {\em volume} of the lattice
 $\mathcal{L}$ is defined to be the positive number
 $|\det{M}|$ which is independent from the basis $\mathcal{B}$. It is denoted 
 by $vol(\mathcal{L})$ or $\det{\mathcal{L}}$ (see also \cite{gal}).
 If ${\bf v}\in \mathbb{R}^m$, then $\|{\bf v}\|$ denotes, as usually, the
 Euclidean norm of ${\bf v}$. 
 We denote by $LLL(M)$, the application of well-known LLL-algorithm on the rows of $M$.
 Finally,   we denote by 
$\lambda_1(\mathcal{L})$ the smallest of the lengths of vectors in 
$ \mathcal{L}-\{ \bf 0\}$.

\section{Closest Vector Problem}\label{sec:CVP}

In this chapter we will define the Closest Vector Problem (CVP)~\cite{gal} and its approximate variation which is the problem we will base our attack on the NTRU system. We will also provide Babai's algorithm~\cite{gal} that solves the approximation CVP problem~\cite{gal}.

\begin{definition}
\textbf{Closest Vector Problem ($CVP(\L,t))$}:  Given a lattice $\mathcal{L}\subset {\mathbb{Z}}^m$ of rank $n$ and a vector $\textbf{t} \in {\mathbb{R}}^m$, $\textbf{t} \notin \L$ , find a vector $\textbf{u} \in \mathcal{L}$ such that, for every ${\textbf{v}} \in \mathcal{L}$ we have:
$$\| \textbf{u} - \textbf{t} \| \leq \| \textbf{v} - \textbf{t} \|$$
\end{definition}

The closest vector problem is known to be NP-hard(Chapter 3 of ~\cite{COMPLEXITY_OF_LATTICE_PROBLEMS}). We will now define the approximate-Closest Vector Problem $(CVP_{\gamma_{n}}({\mathcal{L}},{\bf t}))$ as follows:

\begin{definition}
\textbf{approximate-Closest Vector Problem ($(CVP_{\gamma_{n}}({\mathcal{L}},{\bf t}))$) }: Given a lattice $\mathcal{L}\subset {\mathbb{Z}}^m$ of rank $n$ and a vector ${\bf t}\in {\mathbb{R}}^m,$ find a vector ${\bf u}\in \mathcal{L}$ such that, for every ${\bf v}\in \mathcal{L}$ we have:
$$ \| \textbf{u} - \textbf{t} \| \leq \gamma_n \| \textbf{v} - \textbf{t} \| ( \ for \ some \ \gamma_n \geq 1)$$
\end{definition}

We say that we have a CVP oracle, if we have an efficient probabilistic algorithm that solves ${\rm CVP}_{\gamma_n}$ for $\gamma_n=1.$ To solve 
${\rm{CVP}}_{\gamma_n}$, we usually use Babai's algorithm \cite[Chapter 18]{gal} (which has polynomial running time). In fact, combining this algorithm with LLL algorithm, we solve ${\rm{CVP}}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{L},{\bf t})$ for some lattice $\mathcal{L}\subset {\mathbb{Z}}^m,$ for $\gamma_n = 2^{n/2}$ and $n=rank(\mathcal{L})$ in polynomial time. The pseudocode for Babai's algorithm is given in algorithm \ref{alg:babai}.

\section{Voronoi First Kind Lattices}\label{sec:VFK}

First, we will define the Voronoi Cell and the relevant vectors of a Voronoi First Kind (VFK) Lattice~\cite{} and afterwards we will express the criteria under which a lattice can be considered as VFK. Finally, we will describe an efficient algorithm~\cite{VFK-CVP} to solve the CVP problem for VFK Lattices.

\begin{definition}
% cite https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.00720.pdf, page 2
\textbf{Voronoi cell Vor($\L$)}: Given a lattice $\L \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the voronoi cell $Vor(\L)$ is the set of points closer to the origin than to any other lattice point (with respect to the given norm):
$$ Vor(\L) = \{ \textbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|\textbf{x}\| \leq \| \textbf{x}-\textbf{v} \|\}, \forall \textbf{v} \in \L $$
\end{definition}

Vectors $\textbf{v}$ for which there exists a point that is closer to $ \textbf{v}$ and the origin $0$ than to any other lattice vector, are called \textbf{relevant vectors} and are denoted by $Rel(\textbf{v})$. We are now ready to define the VFK Lattice.

\begin{definition}
\textbf{Voronoi First Kind (VFK) Lattice}: A lattice $\L$ of dimension $n$ is called Voronoi First Kind (VFK) if and only if there is a lattice basis $\{{\bf v}_i\}_{i=1,...,n}$ and a lattice vector ${\bf v}_{0}$ such that:\\

$({\bf i}).$ $\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bf v}_i ={\bf 0}, $ (superbasis condition)\\

$({\bf ii})$ $q_{ij} = {\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_j \leq 0$ for $i,j=0,1,...,n$ with $i\not =j.$ (obtuse condition)
\end{definition}

The $q_{ij}$ are called the selling parameters~\cite{SELLING}. An important and useful property of VFK Lattices is the way its relevant vectors can be written as the sum of a set of its superbasis vectors:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{i\in I}{\bf b}_i
\label{equation:VFK_REL}
\end{equation}

where I is a strict subset of $ \{ 0,1,..,n \} $ and $I \neq\emptyset$. We can now start explainig the main algorithm that we used for our attacks.








\subsection{CVP efficient algorithm for VFK lattices}

Let $\L$ be a VFK lattice in $\mathbb{R}^m$, with an obtuse superbasis $\{{\bf b}_i\}_{i=0,...,n}$ and \textbf{y} a point in $\mathbb{R}^m$. We also define B, a matrix whose columns are represented by the obtuse superbasis vectors, \textbf{y}, a column vector such that $\textbf{y}=B\textbf{z}$ and $\textbf{x}_0$ a lattice point in $\L$. In order to solve the CVP problem in VFK we need to find $n+1$ integers $\{{ w_i\}_{i=0,...,n}$ that minimize the norm:

\begin{equation}
\Big{\|} \textbf{y} - \sum_{i=0}^{n}{\textbf{b}_i w_i} \Big{\|}^2 = \Big{\|} B(\textbf{z}-w) \Big{\|}^2\label{equation:VFK_CVP}
\end{equation}

Robby G. McKilliam, Alex Grant and I. Vaughan L. Clarkson proposed an interesting iterative procedure~\cite{VFK-CVP} that solves the CVP using the following steps: \\

Step 1: $ \textbf{x}_{k+1} = \textbf{x}_k + \textbf{v}_k$ \\

Step 2: $ \textbf{v}_k = argmin_{\textbf{v} \in Rel(\L) \cup \{0\}} \| \textbf{y}-\textbf{x}_k-\textbf{v} \| $ \\

$\textbf{v}_k$ is the relevant vector that minimizes the norm of Step 2, during the k-th iteration. There might be many relevant vectors that minimize this norm and the choice does not change the outcome of the algorithm. It is proven by the same authors~\cite{VFK-CVP} that after every iteration of the algorithm $ \|\textbf{y}-\textbf{x}_k \| > \| \textbf{y}-\textbf{x}_{k+1}\| $ and that after at most n iterations of the algorithm, the closest vector point will be found. The choosing of $\textbf{x}_0$ influences greatly the number of iterations the algorithm will do. Nevertheless, this part of the algorithm has a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(n)$. If we take into consideration the matrix B, the vector y and the property \eqref{equation:VFK_CVP} then the iterative procedure becomes:\\

Step 1: $ \textbf{x}_{k+1} = B\textbf{u}_{k+1}$ \\

Step 2: $ \textbf{u}_{k+1} = \textbf{u}_k + \textbf{t}_k$ \\

Step 3: $ \textbf{t}_k = argmin_{\textbf{t} \in \{0,1\}^{n+1}} \| B(\textbf{z}-\textbf{u}_k-\textbf{t}) \| $ \\

According to the property \eqref{equation:VFK_REL}, a relevant vector $\textbf{v}$ can be written as $\textbf{v} = \sum_{i \in I}\textbf{t}_i\textbf{b}_i$, thus $\textbf{t}_k$ represents the coefficients of a relevant vector, which is why its values are either 0 or 1.

The maximum number of relevant vectors that a lattice can have is $2^{n+1}-2$, the complexity of which grows exponentialy, therefore another way to find the minimizations was found.

% $v_k$ is found by computing a minimum cut in an undirected flow network. The graph is created because of the VFK lattice property \eqref{equation:VFK_REL}.

We set $p=\textbf{z}-\textbf{u}_k$ and the norm in \eqref{equation:VFK_CVP} becomes:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Big{\|} B(p-\textbf{t}) \Big{\|}^2 & =  \Big{\|} \sum_{i=0}^{n}{\textbf{b}_i(p_i-\textbf{t}_i)} \Big{\|}^2 \\ & = \sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}p_ip_j - 2\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}p_j \textbf{t}_i + \sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}\textbf{t}_i\textbf{t}_j
\end{split}
\label{equation:VFK_CVP_MIN}
\end{equation}

Let $s_i = - 2\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}p_j$ and the above quadratic form becomes: have:

\begin{equation}
Q(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{n}s_i \textbf{t}_i + \sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}\textbf{t}_i\textbf{t}_j
\label{equation:VFK_QT}
\end{equation}

% add citation next to laplacian matrix [5,13]
% see and add citation 44,49,53,12 about the technique with the flow networks and mincuts
Robby G. McKilliam, Alex Grant and I. Vaughan L. Clarkson have shown~\cite{VFK-CVP} that a minimizer can be found in Q(t) by ccomputing a minimum cut in un undirected flow network, since there is an one-to-one correspondence between the obtuse superbasis of the VFK lattice and the Laplacian matrix. 

\begin{definition}
An undirected flow network is a graph $G$ with $n+3$ vertices $v_i$ and edges $e_{ij}$ connecting $v_i$ to $v_j$ with weight $w_{ij}$. The graph is undirected, therefore $w_{ij}=w_{ji}$. The first vertice $v_0$ is called source and the last one, $v_{n+2}$, is the sink.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A cut is a subset $C \subset V$, where $V$ is the set of vertices in a graph, and its complement $\Bar{C} \subset V$ and $v_0 \in C, v_{n+2} \in \Bar{C}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
The weight W of a cut is the sum of the weights on the edges crossing from the vertices in $C$ to the vertices in $\Bar{C}$:
$$W = \sum_{i \in I} \sum_{j \in J} w_{ij}$$
Where $I=\{ i | v_i \in C\}, J=\{ j | v_j \in \Bar{C}\}$. The weight $w_{ii}$ of the edge $e_{ii}$ does not change the value of W.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
Mincut is a $C$ and a $\Bar{C}$ that minimize the weight $W$.
\end{definition}

It was shown in~\cite{VFK-CVP} that the weight $W$ can be represented with the following quadratic form:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
F(t) & = \sum_{i=0}^{n}g_i\textbf{ t}_i - \sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}w_{ij}\textbf{t}_i\textbf{t}_j \\
g_i & = d_i + \sum_{j=0}^{n}w_{ij} \\
d_i & = w_{i,n+2} - w_{0,i}
\end{split}
\label{equation:VFK_FT}
\end{equation}

From the equivalence of \eqref{equation:VFK_QT} and \eqref{equation:VFK_FT} we calculate the weigths of our graph:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
w_{i,j} & = -q_{ij} \ (selling \ parameters) \\
s_i & = d_i + \sum_{j=0}^{n} w_{ij}
\end{split}
\label{equation:VFK_S}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
w_{0,i} & = 0, \ if \ s_i \geq 0, \ else \ w_{0,i} = - s_i \\
w_{i,n+2} & = s_i, \ if \ s_i \geq 0, \ else \  w_{i,n+2} = 0
\end{split}
\label{equation:VFK_WEIGHTS}
\end{equation}

We create vector $\textbf{t}$ from set $C$ like this:
\begin{equation}
\textbf{t}_i = 1, \ if \ i \in C, \ else \  \textbf{t}_i = 0
\label{equation:VFK_WEIGHTS}
\end{equation}

From the above we deduct that if we do a minimum cut in a graph $G$ with $n+3$ vertices and the choose the weights of the edges according to \eqref{equation:VFK_S} and \eqref{equation:VFK_WEIGHTS}, then the vertices that exist in $C$ can create the vector t of Step 3 of the iterative procedure. This minimization is computed in $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ operations. The total complexity of the algorithm is  $\mathcal{O}(n)*\mathcal{O}(n^3)=\mathcal{O}(n^4)$. \\

\section{NTRU}\label{sec:ntru}
Let the polynomial ring ${\mathcal{R}}={\mathbb{Z}}[x]/\langle D(x) \rangle$ for some $D(x)\in {\mathbb{Z}}[x]$ and $\langle D(x) \rangle$ the ideal generated by $D(x).$ We write $*$ for the multiplication in the ring. Also, fix a polynomial $h(x)\in {\mathbb{Z}}[x]$ of degree $N.$ We set, 
\begin{equation}\label{B_h}
B_h = \begin{pmatrix}
-\ h(x)\ - \\
-\ x*h(x)\ - \\
\vdots\\
-\ x^{N-1}*h(x)\ -
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where with $x^{i}*h(x)$ we mean the vector with coordinates the coefficients of the polynomial after multiplication in ${\mathcal{R}}.$ When we write the coefficient vector of $h(x)=a_{N-1}x^{N-1}+\cdots + a_0$ we usually denoted with ${\bf h}=(a_0,...,a_{N-1}).$
Then, multiplication $g(x)*h(x)$ in ${\mathcal{R}}$ can be represented as the multiplication of the row matrix $[{\bf g}]$ and matrix $B_h,$ i.e. $[{\bf g}]B_h.$\\
{\bf{Examples}}. $({\rm i}).$ Let $D(x)=x^3+1$ and $h(x)=x+1.$ Then, the multiplication $(x^2+1)*h(x) = x^2 + x$ in ${\mathcal{R}}$ can be written as 
$$[1,0,1]B_h=[1,0,1]\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&0\\
0&1&1\\
-1&0 &1
\end{bmatrix} = [0,1,1]\rightarrow x+x^2.$$
$({\rm ii}).$ If $D(x)=x^N-1$, then the matrix $B_h$ for some polynomial $h(x)$ in ${\mathcal{R}}$ is the cyclic matrix generated by the coefficients of $h(x).$\\
$({\rm iii}).$ If $D(x)=x^3-x-1$ and $h(x)=x+1$ then $(x^2+1)*h(x)=x^2+2x+2,$ and
$$[1,0,1]B_h=[1,0,1]\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&0\\
0&1&1\\
1&1 &1
\end{bmatrix} = [2,2,1]\rightarrow 2+2x+x^2.$$
The set 
$${\mathcal{L}}_h=\{(f(x),g(x))\in {\mathcal{R}}^2 : g(x)=f(x)*h(x)\}$$ where $h$ has degree say $N$, is a lattice. Indeed,
$${\mathcal{L}}_h={\mathbb{Z}}^{2N}B_h'$$ where 
$$B'_h=		\left[\begin{array}{c}
			 B_h  \\
			\hline
			qI_N   \\
		\end{array}\right].
			$$
If you consider the previous lattice, but taking ${\mod{q}}$ (for some positive $q$) we get a (NTRU type) lattice
$${\mathcal{L}}_h^q=\{(f(x),g(x))\in {\mathcal{R}}^2 : g(x)=f(x)*h(x)\pmod{q}\}$$ where we also write it as,
$${\mathcal{L}}_h^q=\{({\bf f},{\bf g})\in {\mathbb{Z}}^{2N} : [{\bf g}]=[{\bf f}]M_h\}$$
where 
$$	M_h=\left[\begin{array}{c|c}
			I_N & B_h  \\
			\hline
			{\bf 0}_N & qI_N   \\
		\end{array}\right].
			$$
The lattice ${\mathcal{L}}_h$ has several interesting properties when $B_h$ is a cyclic matrix (see (\ref{B_h})). One example is when we choose $h(x)=x^N-1$. In this case, if $({\bf a},{\bf b})$ is a vector in the lattice, then performing a cyclic permutation of ${\bf a}$ and ${\bf b}$ $k-$times will result in another vector in the lattice.
\subsection{NTRU}
We first define a Key Encapsulation Mechanism (KEM) which consists from three algorithms.\\
{\it{keyGen}}. A probabilistic algorithm that outputs a public key({\it{pk}}) and a secret key({\it{sk}}). \\
{\it{Encap}}. A probabilistic algorithm that takes as input the public key and outputs a ciphertext($c$) and a shared secret($k$). \\
{\it{Decap}}. A probabilistic algorithm that takes as input the ciphertext and the secret key, and outputs the shared secret.\\

Let $q,N$ be positive integers with $N$ prime. A ternary polynomial is one that has as coefficients only the integers $-1,0,1.$  With 
${\mathcal{T}}_a$ we denote the set of ternary polynomials of ${\mathcal{R}}$ with degree at most $a$ and $\mathcal{T}_a(d_1,d_2)\subset {\mathcal{T}}_{a}$ consists from elements of ${\mathcal{T}}_{a}$ with $d_1$ coefficients equal to $1$ and $d_2$ equal to $-1.$ Furthermore, with ${\mathcal{T}}_a(w), w\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}$ we denote the ternary polynomials which have $w$ non-zero coefficients. With ${\mathcal{T}}_{a,+}$ we denote the subset of ${\mathcal{T}}_a$ consisting from polynomials $f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha}v_ix^i$ such that $\sum_{i=0}^{a-1}v_iv_{i+1}\ge 0.$	Let $D(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $N.$ We also have four sample spaces,
${\mathcal{L}}_f, {\mathcal{L}}_g, {\mathcal{L}}_r,$ and ${\mathcal{L}}_m.$
We set, $\Phi_1(x)=x-1$ and in the case of $D(x)=x^N-1,$ we set $\Phi_N(x) = D(x)/\Phi_1(x).$  
What is more, we set \\\\
$-$ ${\mathcal{R}}={\mathbb{Z}}[x]/\langle D(x) 
\rangle$, ${\mathcal{R}/3}={\mathbb{Z}}_3[x]/
\langle D(x) \rangle$ and  
${\mathcal{R}}/q={\mathbb{Z}}_q[x]/\langle D(x) 
\rangle$. \\\\
$-$ ${\mathcal{S}}={\mathbb{Z}}[x]/\langle \Phi_N(x) 
\rangle$, ${\mathcal{S}/3}={\mathbb{Z}}_3[x]/\langle \Phi_N(x)  \rangle$ 
and  ${\mathcal{S}}/q={\mathbb{Z}}_q[x]/\langle \Phi_N(x) \rangle.$
\subsection{Generation of Public key}
We choose the {\it {secret key}}, $(g(x), f(x))$ from ${\mathcal{L}}_g$ and ${\mathcal{L}}_f$, respectively, which both are suitable subsets of ${\mathcal{T}}_{a}$ (for some $a$). Also $g(x)$ is invertible in ${\mathcal{R}}/3.$  The {\it {public key}} is $h(x)=g(x)/(\varepsilon f(x))$ invertible in ${\mathcal{R}}/q,$ where $\varepsilon=1/3$ or $3.$ Since there are many different sample spaces in the bibliography, here for NTRU-HPS and NTRU-HRSS, we follow \cite{nist2-ntru} and for NTRU-prime we follow \cite{ntru-prime}. In all the flavors we ignore encoding/decoding operations.

\section{Auxiliary Results}
	Let $k, N$ and $q$ be positive integers.	
We set 	
	$$M_k=
		\left[\begin{array}{c|c}
		I_N & -kI_N  \\
		\hline
		{\bf 0}_N & qI_N   \\
		\end{array}\right].
		$$ 
Let ${\mathcal{L}}_{k,q,N}$ be the lattice generated by the rows of $M_k.$ We write ${\mathcal{L}}_{k}$ for the previous lattice.

Before we present the Proposition we remark that if $k=q$ the lattice is VFK. Indeed, we can check that the rows produce an obtuse superbasis,
\[{\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_j= 0\ {\rm and}\ {\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_{N+j}\le 0,\ (1\le i<j\le N),\]
and ${\bf v}_0=-{\bf v}_1-\cdots-{\bf v}_{2N}=(-1,-1,...,-1;0,0,...,0),$ is such that 
$${\bf v}_0\cdot {\bf v}_j\le 0\ \ (j=1,2,...,2N).$$

\begin{proposition}
If $k\leq \sqrt{2q-1},$ then the lattice ${\mathcal{L}}_k$ is VFK.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We consider the unimodular matrix $U_P\in GL_{2N}({\mathbb{Z}})$ (for some integer $P$), 
\begin{equation}\label{matrix_change_basis}
U_P=\left[\begin{array}{c|c}
	I_N & {\bf 0}_N  \\
	\hline
	PI_N & I_N   \\
\end{array}\right]=
\left[\begin{array}{cccccccc}
1 &     0 & \dots &   0      &  0   &  0   & \dots & 0  \\
0&     1 & \dots &   0       &   0&  0   & \dots & 0  \\
\vdots & \vdots  &  \ddots & \vdots &   \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots   \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 1            &   0    &   0  &  \dots &   0 \\
P & 0 &  \dots &    0        &     1     & 0     &  \dots &    0    \\
0 & P &  \dots &    0        &     0     & 1   &  \dots &    0    \\
\vdots & \vdots  &  \ddots & \vdots &   \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots   \\
0 & 0 &  \dots &    P        &     0     & 0   &  \dots &    1    \\
\end{array}\right].
\end{equation} 
		
We shall prove that we can find an integer $P$ such that, the rows of the matrix $U_PM_k$ is an obtuse superbasis of ${\mathcal{L}}.$ This integer $P$ will depend on $k$ and $q,$

We first compute the matrix
$$A = U_PM_k=$$
\begin{equation}\label{vfk-matrix}
 \left[\begin{array}{c|c}
I_N & -kI_N  \\
\hline
PI_N & (q-Pk)I_N   \\
\end{array}\right].
\end{equation}
The superbasis is 
$$	
\begin{array}{ccc}
{\bf v}_1 & = &(1,0,...,0;-k,0,...,0)\\
{\bf v}_2 & =  & (0,1,...,0; 0,-k,...,0)\\
... \\
{\bf v}_N & = &(0,0,...,1;0,0,...,-k) \\
{\bf v}_{N+1} & = &(P,0,...,0;q-Pk,0,...,0) \\
...\\
{\bf v}_{2N} & = & (0,0,...,P;0,0,...,q-Pk)\\
\end{array}
$$ 
and
$${\bf v}_0 = -\sum_{j=1}^{2N} {\bf v}_j=(-1-P,...,-1-P;kP+k-q,...,kP+k-q).$$
Since ${\bf v}_i$ and ${\bf v}_{i+N}$ have non zero elements only at positions $i, N+i$ we conclude that
$${\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_{j+N}=0,\ (i\not=j,\ 1\leq i,j\leq N).$$
Further,
\begin{equation}\label{vfk_inequality_1}
{\bf v}_1\cdot {\bf v}_{N+1} = \cdots={\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_{i+N}=\cdots=(kP - q)k + P, \ (1\leq i \leq N).
\end{equation}			 
Similarly, 
$${\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_j=0, \ \ {\bf v}_{i+N}\cdot {\bf v}_{j+N}=0,\ (1\leq i<j\leq N),$$
\begin{equation}\label{vfk_inequality_2}
{\bf v}_0\cdot {\bf v}_i =  -(kP + k - q)k - P - 1,\ (1\leq i\leq N),
\end{equation}			 
and
\begin{equation}\label{vfk_inequality_3}
{\bf v}_0\cdot {\bf v}_{i+N} =  (kP + k - q)(q-kP) - (P + 1)P,\ \ (1\leq i\leq N).
\end{equation}		
From (\ref{vfk_inequality_1}), in order to have ${\bf v}_i\cdot {\bf v}_{i+N}\leq 0$ \ $(1\le i\le n),$  we get 
$$P\le \beta=\frac{kq}{k^2+1}$$
and similarly from (\ref{vfk_inequality_2}) we get $$P\ge \alpha=\frac{kq-k^2-1}{k^2+1}.$$
Remark that,	
 $\beta - \alpha = 1.$ So, there is at least one   integer value in 
$[\alpha,\beta],$ the value 
\begin{equation}\label{value_of_P}
P=\lfloor kq/(k^2+1)\rfloor.
\end{equation}
Assume that $\beta\not\in {\mathbb{Z}}.$
Else, $P=\beta.$ 
Consider the polynomial, 
$$g(x) = -x^2(1+k^2)+x(-k^2+2kq-1)+q(k-q).$$
Therefore, it is enough the relation (\ref{vfk_inequality_3}) be non positive at $P$, i.e. $g(P)\leq 0.$
But the discriminant of $g(x)$ equals to $\Delta = (k^2+1)^2-4q^2.$ So, to have $g(x)\leq 0$ it is enough $\Delta\leq 0,$ therefore we conclude $(1+k^2)^2\le 4q^2.$ The result follows.
\end{proof}

\section{Attack}
{\bf Idea}. For NTRU-prime we set $N=p$ and $m(x)=\texttt{round}(h(x)*r(x)) - h(x)*r(x).$ In ${\mathcal{R}}/q$ we have $c(x)=r(x)*h(x)+m(x).$ Multiplying both sides with $-k$ we get 
$km(x)=b(x)+u(x),$
where $u(x)=-kr(x)*h(x)$ and $b(x)=kc(x).$
We set ${\bf V}$ be the unknown vector $({\bf m},{\bf u}).$ Assume that we can find a vector ${\bf E}\in {\mathbb{Z}}^{2N}$ such that,
$$||{\bf V}-{\bf E}||<\frac{1}{2}\lambda_1.$$
We set 
$${\bf t}=(E_1,...,E_N,b_1+E_{N+1},...,b_N+E_{2N}).$$
We remark that 
$({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u})$ is in ${\mathcal{L}}_k.$ 
Indeed,
$$({\bf m},{\bf v})M_k=
({\bf m},{\bf v})\left[\begin{array}{c|c}
			I_N & -kI_N  \\
			\hline
			{\bf 0}_N & qI_N   \\
		\end{array}\right] = 	 ({\bf m},-k{\bf m}+q{\bf v})=({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u}).$$
Then, ${\bf W}\leftarrow BDD({\mathcal{L}}_k,{\bf t})$  provides the message ${\bf m}.$ Indeed,
$$||{\bf W} - {\bf t}||\le ||({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u})-{\bf t}||=||{\bf V}-{\bf E}||<\frac{1}{2}\lambda_1.$$
Finally, 
$$||{\bf W}-({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u})||\le ||{\bf W}-{\bf t}|| +  ||({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u}) - {\bf t}||<\lambda_1.$$
But, ${\bf W}-({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u})\in {\mathcal{L}}_k$ thus, ${\bf W}=({\bf m},{\bf b}+{\bf u})$. We conclude therefore that the first $N-$coordinates of ${\bf W}$ provide the message ${\bf m}.$

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{flow\_network}\label{alg:GRAPH}
\SetKwInOut{Input}{input}
\SetKwInOut{Output}{output}
\Input{target vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1 \times 1}$, $u \in \mathbb{N}^{n+1 \times 1} $, selling parameters $q_{ij}$}
\Output{An undirected flow network $G$}
$p \gets z - u$\;
Create Graph $G$ with $n+3$ nodes and edges connecting them\;
source $\gets$ first node\;
sink $\gets$ last node\;
Edges $e_{ij}$ have the following weights: \\
\For{$i=1$, \textbf{to} $n+1$}{
\For{$j=i+1$, \textbf{to} $n+1$}{
$w_{ij} = - q_{ij}$\;
}
$s_i = -2 \sum_{j=1}^{n+1} q_{ij} p_j$\;
\eIf{$s_i \geq 0$}{
    $w_{i,sink} \gets s_i$\;
    $w_{source,i} \gets 0$\;
}{
    $w_{i,sink} \gets 0$ \;
    $w_{source,i} \gets -s_i$\;
}
}
\Return{$G$}
\end{algorithm}

The minimum cut algorithm of \ref{alg:MINCUT}, uses the Ford-Fulkerson procedure~\cite{MAXFLOW} for undirected graphs, that finds the max-flow of a graph, which is equivalent to the minimum cut~\cite{MAXFLOWMINCUT}. $f(i,j), i,j \in E(G)$ describes the flow from the node $i$ to node $j$. In our experiments we are using the implimentation of networkx's mincut algorithm \footnote{\url{https://networkx.org/}}.

\SetKwComment{Comment}{/* }{ */}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{mincut}\label{alg:MINCUT}
\SetKwInOut{Input}{input}
\SetKwInOut{Output}{output}
\Input{Graph $G$, source node $s$, sink node $t$}
\Output{vector $t \in \{0,1\}^{n+1}$}

\For{each edge $(u,v) \in E(G)$}{
 $f(u,v) \gets 0$\;
 $f(v,u) \gets 0$\;
}
\While{ $\exists$ a path $p$ from $s$ to $t$ with no cycles in the residual network $G_f$}{
    $c_f(p) \gets min\{ c_f(u,v) : (u,v) \in p \}$ \Comment*[r]{residual capacity}
    \For{ each edge $(u,v) \in p$}{
    $f(u,v) \gets f(u,v) + c_f(p)$\;
    $f(v,u) \gets - f(u,v)$\;
    }
}
$C \gets$ all the vertices that exist on the max-flow path\;
\For{$i=1$, \textbf{to} $n+1$}{
\eIf{$v_i \in C$}{
    $t_i=1$\;
}{
    $t_i=0$\;
}
}
\Return{t}
\end{algorithm}
